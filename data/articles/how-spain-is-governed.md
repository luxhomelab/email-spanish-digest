Who actually runs Spain? The King? The prime minister? Parliament? If you read Spanish news for long enough, you'll see all three described as making decisions, plus a Constitutional Court that can overturn laws and seventeen regional governments with their own parliaments.

This guide untangles it: who does what, who gets elected and how often, and how the pieces keep each other in check.

## It all starts with the 1978 Constitution

Spain's current system rests on one document: the [Constitution](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229) approved by referendum on **6 December 1978**, three years after Franco's death and after almost four decades of dictatorship. Article 1 sets out three ideas that everything else follows from:

- **Rule of law:** Spain is a social and democratic state under the rule of law (*Estado social y democrático de Derecho*).
- **Popular sovereignty:** sovereignty belongs to the Spanish people, so power comes from voters, not from the crown.
- **Parliamentary monarchy:** the form of government (*Monarquía parlamentaria*): a king as head of state, a government chosen through parliament.

That last word, *parliamentary*, is the key to the rest of this article. Spain has a king, but the people who govern are chosen through parliament.

## Three branches of power, and a King above them

Like most democracies, Spain splits power three ways:

- **Legislative:** the Cortes Generales (parliament) pass laws and the budget.
- **Executive:** the Government (*Gobierno*) runs the country.
- **Judicial:** judges and courts (*Jueces y Tribunales*) administer justice.

The King sits above all three as head of state and arbiter — but he is not a branch of power himself.

## The King reigns, but does not rule

Spain's head of state is **King Felipe VI**, on the throne since 2014. Nobody votes for him: the crown is hereditary.

On paper his list of duties looks powerful. He signs and promulgates laws, convenes and dissolves parliament, calls elections, nominates the candidate for prime minister, is supreme commander of the armed forces and represents Spain abroad.

The catch is a mechanism called *el refrendo* (countersignature). Almost every act the King signs must be countersigned by the prime minister or a minister, and they — not the King — bear political responsibility for it. In practice, he formalises decisions others have already taken.

> TAKE: The King is the symbol of the state and its formal arbiter. Real political decisions are made by the Government and parliament, which answer to voters.

::: graphic the-king-countersignature

## Parliament: the Cortes Generales

Spain's parliament, the **Cortes Generales**, has two chambers: the [Congress of Deputies](https://www.congreso.es) and the [Senate](https://www.senado.es).

> KEY: Congress seats **350** elected every 4 years

> KEY: Absolute majority **176** the number to remember

> KEY: Senate seats **≈266** 209 elected + ≈57 regional

The **Congress of Deputies** (*Congreso de los Diputados*) has 350 seats, elected every four years by proportional representation, province by province. The number to remember is **176**: an absolute majority. It comes up again and again — to elect a prime minister in the first vote, and to remove one.

The **Senate** is a mix: **209 senators are elected directly** (generally four per province, and from the 2026 election one for Formentera), and **around 57 more are appointed by the regional parliaments**, so the exact size shifts slightly with population.

Both chambers pass laws, but Congress always has the final word.

::: graphic cortes-generales

## Congress vs Senate: two chambers, unequal power

Why have two chambers at all? Because they do very different jobs.

- **How you vote:** Congress — closed party lists. Senate — you tick named candidates (up to three per province).
- **Choosing the PM:** Congress only, through the investiture vote.
- **Bringing the government down:** Congress only, through a motion of censure.
- **Laws:** the Senate can amend or veto a bill, but Congress can override it.
- **The Senate's one big power:** approving Article 155, which lets the central state step in when a region breaks the law (used once, in Catalonia in 2017).

Both chambers are elected on the same day, for the same four years — but only Congress decides who governs.

## How a law is made, from bill to BOE

Under [Article 87](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a87) of the Constitution, five kinds of actor can put a bill before parliament:

- **The Government:** a *proyecto de ley*, approved by the Council of Ministers. Most laws start here.
- **Congress:** a *proposición de ley*, signed by a parliamentary group or by 15 deputies.
- **The Senate:** also a *proposición*, from a group or 25 senators. It still goes to Congress first.
- **Regional parliaments:** they can send a bill to Congress, or ask the Government to draft one.
- **Citizens:** 500,000 verified signatures, the *iniciativa legislativa popular*. It can't be used for organic laws, taxes, international affairs or pardons.

**Does a law have to pass both chambers?** Yes. Every law goes through both, and always in the same order: Congress first, then the Senate. Under [Article 90](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a90), the Senate has **two months** (20 days for a bill declared urgent) to pass it, amend it by simple majority, or veto it by absolute majority. Then it goes back to Congress, which votes on each of the Senate's amendments separately. An amendment stays in the text if it wins a simple majority ([Congress Rules, Article 123](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1982-5196#a123)). A veto on an ordinary law can be overturned straight away with 176 votes, or by a simple majority once two months have passed. A veto on an organic law always needs 176. So the Senate can slow a law down, but it can't kill it.

Some laws need more votes. **Organic laws** (*leyes orgánicas*), which cover fundamental rights, the regions' statutes of autonomy and the electoral system, need **176 votes** in Congress in a final vote on the whole text ([Article 81](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a81)). If the Senate has amended one, the final text goes to that 176-vote test again ([Congress Rules, Article 132](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1982-5196#a132)).

**When does it become law?** Once the Cortes have passed it, the King has **15 days** to sign it (*sancionar*) and must order it published straight away ([Article 91](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a91)). Publication is in the [Boletín Oficial del Estado](https://www.boe.es) (BOE), the state's official gazette. Unless the law sets its own date, it enters into force **20 days after publication** ([Civil Code, Article 2](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1889-4763#a2)). In practice, many laws set their own date, often the day after publication.

> TIP: A law's name tells you its history. *Ley 12/2023, de 24 de mayo, por el derecho a la vivienda* is the 12th law of 2023, signed on 24 May. On boe.es every law has a "consolidated" version with all later amendments already worked in, like the [housing law](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2023-12203).

## How the prime minister is chosen

This is the part that surprises most newcomers: **Spaniards do not vote for their prime minister directly.** They vote for parties, and the prime minister (officially *Presidente del Gobierno*) is whoever can win a vote in Congress.

The process runs in five steps:

- **1. Election:** citizens vote for party lists (*elecciones generales*).
- **2. 350 seats:** seats in Congress are shared out among parties.
- **3. The King:** he consults the leader of every party with seats (*ronda de consultas*) and nominates a candidate.
- **4. Investiture:** the candidate needs 176 votes in the first vote. If they fail, 48 hours later a simple majority — more yes than no — is enough.
- **5. Prime minister:** the King formally appoints the winner, who then picks the ministers.

If no candidate wins within **two months** of the first investiture vote, parliament is automatically dissolved and a new election is called. That's what happened in 2016 and again in 2019.

How steps 1 and 2 actually work, from the 52 electoral districts to the d'Hondt formula and why a blank vote can knock a small party out, is explained step by step in our guide to [how Spain votes](/articles/how-spain-votes).

::: graphic investiture

> TIP: When Spanish news talks about "pactos" or "negociaciones" after an election, this is why: with no party holding 176 seats on its own, the candidate has to collect enough yes votes (or abstentions) from other parties to pass the investiture.

## The Government, kept in check

The Government is the **Council of Ministers** ([*Consejo de Ministros*](https://www.lamoncloa.gob.es)): the prime minister, any deputy prime ministers and the ministers. The PM appoints and dismisses ministers freely. The Government directs domestic and foreign policy, defence and public administration, drafts the budget, and can pass emergency **decree-laws** — which Congress must approve within **30 days** or they lapse (more on those below).

Congress holds it to account in three ways:

- **Motion of censure:** the *moción de censura* must be constructive — 176 votes and a named replacement PM. That is exactly how the government changed in 2018.
- **Confidence vote:** in a *cuestión de confianza*, the PM asks Congress to reaffirm its support.
- **Control sessions:** every week, in the *sesiones de control*, ministers answer MPs' questions in the chamber.

And it works both ways: the prime minister can ask the King to **dissolve parliament early** and call a snap election.

::: graphic motion-of-censure

## Decree-laws: the Government's shortcut

Going through both chambers takes months. For emergencies, [Article 86](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a86) lets the Government skip the queue. "In case of extraordinary and urgent need", the Council of Ministers can approve a **royal decree-law** (*real decreto-ley*). It has the force of law as soon as it takes effect, usually the day after it appears in the BOE.

There are two limits:

- **What it can touch:** not the basic institutions of the state, the rights and duties in Title I of the Constitution, the regions' powers or electoral law.
- **How long it lasts on its own:** Congress must vote on it within **30 days** of its promulgation. It is a single yes-or-no vote on the whole text, with no amendments. Yes is a *convalidación*. No is a *derogación*, and the decree stops applying from that day. The Senate takes no part.

A validated decree stays in force. Congress can also vote to process it as an ordinary bill afterwards, which opens it up to amendments.

**And if parliament has been dissolved?** Decree-laws don't stop. Once the chambers are dissolved, every bill still in progress lapses ([Congress Rules, Article 207](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1982-5196#a207)). Decree-laws are the exception. They go to the **Diputación Permanente** ([Article 78](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a78)), a standing committee that keeps parliament's emergency powers alive until the new Cortes meet. Its seats are shared out among the groups in proportion to their size: at least 21 members, and its exact size is set by each legislature (Congress's committee had [69 members](https://www.congreso.es/es/diputacion-permanente) in 2026). The Government, meanwhile, keeps its full powers until election day: it only becomes a caretaker government (*en funciones*) once the election has been held ([Article 101](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a101)).

::: graphic decree-law

## A real example: dissolve, then decree again

These rules can be combined in ways that aren't obvious when you read them one by one. In autumn 2026 a government used them to bring back measures that Congress had just voted down. The dates and numbers below come from that case, but the mechanism works the same way whenever parliament is dissolved.

On 29 September 2026, after an 87-year-old woman was evicted in Madrid and protests spread, the Government approved two housing decree-laws ([Royal Decree-law 26/2026](https://www.boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-2026-20266) and [27/2026](https://www.boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-2026-20385)). They suspended evictions of vulnerable tenants, extended expiring rental contracts and limited investment funds' purchases of homes. On 2 October Congress [threw both out](https://www.boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-2026-20526), by [178 votes to 172](https://www.europapress.es/economia/construccion-y-vivienda-00342/noticia-congreso-rechaza-decreto-medidas-vivienda-votos-pp-vox-junts-upn-20261002153000.html), with PP, Vox, Junts and UPN voting no. What followed was legal, and every step was published in the BOE:

- **Dissolution:** on 5 October, at an extraordinary Council of Ministers, Pedro Sánchez proposed dissolving the Cortes. Under [Article 115](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a115) that is the prime minister's call alone, once the cabinet has deliberated. The King signed [Royal Decree 806/2026](https://www.boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-2026-20742), which set the election for 29 November.
- **New decrees:** the next day, the Council of Ministers approved two new housing decree-laws covering largely the same ground, with some changes ([28/2026](https://www.boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-2026-20822) and [29/2026](https://www.boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-2026-20823)).
- **A different room:** with Congress dissolved, the new decrees no longer went to the full chamber of 350 but to the Diputación Permanente, which then had 69 members. Seats are rounded group by group, and that shifted the balance. The groups that had voted no held 34 seats (PP 27, Vox 6, Junts 1). The groups that had voted yes held 35. UPN had no seat at all.
- **Staggered start dates:** 29/2026 took effect at once, on 8 October. 28/2026, which made landlords who won't renew a lease pay compensation, was set to start only on 15 November. According to [Heraldo](https://www.heraldo.es/noticias/economia/2026/10/15/oficial-nuevo-decreto-contempla-indemnizaciones-inquilinos-casero-no-renueva-alquiler-2057837.html), the delay was meant to let the committee vote before it applied to anyone.

Is it allowed to bring back a decree that Congress has just rejected? Nothing in the Constitution forbids it. The preamble of the new decree lists earlier cases where a government did the same, going back to 2006. Critics see it as a way around a parliamentary defeat. In this case, Andalusia's regional government announced it would [challenge the decrees](https://www.eldiario.es/andalucia/gobierno-moreno-recurrira-constitucional-decreto-maricarmen-suspende-desahucios-personas-vulnerables_1_13588021.html) at the Constitutional Court.

> TAKE: Dissolving parliament ends every bill in progress, but not the Government's power to issue decree-laws. With the chambers dissolved, those decrees are voted on by a smaller committee, where the numbers can come out differently.

## The courts: judges answer only to the law

The core principle of the judiciary is simple: judges are bound only by the law — not by the Government and not by parliament.

Ordinary courts form a pyramid. Most cases start in the **courts of first instance** at the bottom, go up through the **Provincial Courts** (*Audiencias Provinciales*) and the **National High Court** and **Regional High Courts** (*Audiencia Nacional*, *TSJ*), with the **Supreme Court** ([*Tribunal Supremo*](https://www.poderjudicial.es)) at the top.

The judiciary is run by the **General Council of the Judiciary** (CGPJ): 20 members on five-year terms, 10 elected by Congress and 10 by the Senate, each by a three-fifths majority. It appoints and promotes judges and oversees discipline. The **Attorney General** (*Fiscal General del Estado*) is proposed by the Government and appointed by the King after hearing the CGPJ.

::: graphic courts

## The Constitutional Court

Separate from the ordinary courts stands the **Constitutional Court** ([*Tribunal Constitucional*](https://www.tribunalconstitucional.es)), the guardian of the Constitution.

It has **12 judges**: four chosen by Congress, four by the Senate, two by the Government and two by the CGPJ. They serve **nine-year terms**, and a third of the court is renewed every three years, so it never flips all at once with a change of political majority. The King formally appoints them.

Its three jobs:

- Reviewing whether laws comply with the Constitution — and **striking them down** if they don't.
- Protecting citizens' fundamental rights on appeal (*recurso de amparo*).
- Settling disputes between the central state and the regions.

::: graphic constitutional-court

## Who elects and controls whom

Put it all together and you get a web where every institution is chosen or checked by another:

- **Citizens** elect the Cortes every four years — but not the prime minister directly.
- **The Cortes** invest the Government, scrutinise it and can censure it.
- **The Government** can trigger an early dissolution of parliament (via the King).
- **Parliament** elects all 20 members of the CGPJ and 8 of the 12 constitutional judges.
- **The Constitutional Court** can strike down any law parliament passes.

> Nobody has the last word on everything. That is the whole point of the design.

::: graphic checks-and-balances

## Power is split vertically too

Spain is a "State of Autonomies". Beyond Madrid, power is shared out among:

> KEY: Autonomous communities **17** each with its own parliament

> KEY: Autonomous cities **2** Ceuta and Melilla

> KEY: Municipalities **≈8,100** plus 50 provinces

And the same pattern repeats at every level:

- **Country:** citizens elect Congress, which picks the prime minister. It handles defence, foreign policy and pensions.
- **Region:** citizens elect a regional parliament, which picks the regional president. It handles healthcare, education and, in some regions, policing.
- **Town:** citizens elect councillors, who choose the mayor. It handles streets, transport, waste and permits.

That is why your doctor's appointment depends on your region's health service, while your *empadronamiento* is done at the town hall.

::: graphic three-levels

## How often Spaniards vote

- **General elections:** at least every four years, and sometimes early — like the snap election called for 29 November 2026. See [how Spain votes](/articles/how-spain-votes) for how those votes become seats.
- **Regional elections:** every four years; dates vary by region.
- **Municipal elections:** every four years, in May, on the same day across Spain.
- **European elections:** every five years, for 61 MEPs.

Some key posts are not chosen by voters at all: the King (for life, hereditary), the CGPJ (five years, elected by the Cortes), the Constitutional Court (nine years, a third every three years) and the Ombudsman (*Defensor del Pueblo*, five years, elected by the Cortes).

## The big picture

Power in Spain is shared so it can't pile up in one place. Parliament makes the laws, the Government runs the country, and the courts apply the law. The King is the symbol and the arbiter, and the Constitution stands above them all.

Once you know that the prime minister needs Congress, that Congress needs 176 votes to change anything big, and that the regions run most day-to-day services, Spanish political news becomes a lot easier to follow. ## FAQ

### Do Spaniards vote for the prime minister directly?

No. Spaniards vote for party lists in Congress. After the election, the King consults party leaders and nominates a candidate, who must win an investiture vote in Congress: 176 votes in the first round, or a simple majority 48 hours later.

### What does the King of Spain actually do?

The King is head of state: he signs laws, calls elections, nominates the candidate for prime minister and represents Spain abroad. But almost every act he signs is countersigned by the Government, which takes political responsibility. He reigns but does not rule.

### What is the difference between the Congress and the Senate?

Congress (350 seats) chooses the prime minister, can remove the Government with a motion of censure and has the final word on laws. The Senate (209 elected plus about 57 appointed by regions) can amend or veto bills, but Congress can override it. The Senate's key own power is approving Article 155 interventions in a region.

### How can a Spanish government be removed?

Through a constructive motion of censure in Congress: it needs 176 votes and must name a replacement prime minister at the same time. This happened in 2018. A government can also fall if the PM loses a confidence vote they called themselves.

### What does the Constitutional Court do?

It checks that laws respect the 1978 Constitution and can strike them down, protects fundamental rights on appeal, and settles disputes between the state and the regions. It has 12 judges serving nine-year terms, with a third renewed every three years.

### How many regions does Spain have?

Spain has 17 autonomous communities and 2 autonomous cities (Ceuta and Melilla), plus 50 provinces and around 8,100 municipalities. Each autonomous community has its own statute, parliament and government.

### Who can propose a law in Spain?

The Government (a *proyecto de ley*, approved by the Council of Ministers), Congress (a group or 15 deputies), the Senate (a group or 25 senators), the regional parliaments, and citizens with 500,000 verified signatures. Popular initiatives can't cover organic laws, taxes, international affairs or pardons.

### Does a law have to pass both the Congress and the Senate?

Yes. Every law goes through Congress first and then the Senate, which has two months to approve, amend or veto it. But Congress has the last word: it votes on each Senate amendment by simple majority, and can overturn a Senate veto with 176 votes straight away. For an ordinary law, a simple majority is also enough once two months have passed.

### When does a new Spanish law come into force?

After both chambers pass it, the King signs it within 15 days and it is published in the BOE (Boletín Oficial del Estado). Unless the law sets its own date, it enters into force 20 days after publication. Royal decree-laws usually take effect the day after publication.

### Can the Spanish government pass laws after parliament is dissolved?

It can't pass ordinary laws, because all pending bills lapse when the Cortes are dissolved. It can still approve royal decree-laws in an emergency. These must be validated within 30 days by the Diputación Permanente, the standing committee that stands in for Congress until the new chambers meet. This is how the October 2026 housing decrees were reissued after the snap election was called.
