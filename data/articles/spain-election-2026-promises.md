Spain holds an early general election on **Sunday, 29 November 2026**. Twelve national and regional parties have published a programme. Together they make **865 promises** about rent, income tax, the Beckham regime, residence permits, pensions, nuclear power and much more. We read all of them and asked the same question about each one: could a new government really do this?

This guide is for people who live in Spain, whether or not they can vote here. Most foreign residents cannot vote in a general election. The Constitution keeps that right for Spanish citizens ([art. 13](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a13)), and EU citizens can vote only in local and European elections. Still, the next government will set many rules that affect your life, from your rental contract to your tax return. All information here is **as of 23 November 2026**, six days before the vote. Our guide to [how Spain votes](/articles/how-spain-votes) explains how votes become seats, and [how Spain is governed](/articles/how-spain-is-governed) explains what happens after the election.

::: toc

## Key takeaways

- **Programmes of small steps score high, and programmes of big change score low.** PP, PSOE and Coalición Canaria have 81 to 86% of their promises rated realistic. Sumar, Adelante Andalucía, Vox and BNG are at 34 to 42%. This shows how hard the promises are to carry out. It does not show whether they are good.
- **Eight parties promise to change the Constitution**: PSOE, Sumar, Podemos, ERC, EH Bildu, BNG, Adelante Andalucía and Vox. Each change needs a three-fifths or two-thirds majority. In practice this has always needed both PP and PSOE.
- **176 votes decide a lot.** Changes to the immigration law, the judges' council and the "gag law" need an organic law. An organic law needs an absolute majority: 176 of the 350 deputies.
- **The regions control housing and health.** Many national promises on rent, building and waiting lists really mean "more money, and the regions must agree".
- **EU law sets firm limits.** This is true especially for rules that treat people differently by nationality, for VAT and for the free movement of money, which also protects buyers from outside the EU.
- **Almost every party promises more than its planned income can pay for.** Spain is also still using an extended budget from 2023 ([Finance Ministry](https://www.hacienda.gob.es/es-ES/Prensa/Noticias/Paginas/2025/20251223-NP-CM-PRORROGA-PGE.aspx)).

## How to read the ratings

Every promise has one of five ratings. It also has a chance of happening in the next four years and a list of what it needs. We give a **D** only when the law stops a promise: it goes against the Constitution, EU law or an international treaty, or it needs a change to the Constitution that one of the two largest parties, PP or PSOE, opposes. When only money or votes are missing, the promise gets a **C**, even if the chance is small.

> TAKE: A high rating does not mean a policy is good. A small technical change scores higher than a big reform only because it is easier to carry out. We use the same tests for every party, national or regional, right or left.

## What it could mean for you

These are the changes that matter most if you rent, own a home, work or have a residence permit in Spain. Each topic links to its promises in the explorer. There you can open every promise to see its rating, its pros and cons, and its sources.

### Housing and rent

On housing, the parties are very far apart. **PP and Vox** want to cancel the 2023 Housing Law (*Ley de Vivienda*). This law lets regions name "stressed market areas" (*zonas tensionadas*), where rents have a limit. If both parties vote for it, an ordinary law is enough to cancel it. **PSOE** wants to stop rents in those areas from going above the official price index. **Sumar** and **Podemos** go much further. Sumar wants rents linked to local incomes, which we rate unlikely, and **Adelante Andalucía** wants rent of no more than 20% of local income, which we rate unlikely too. Podemos wants a 40% rent cut by law, which we rate not possible.

Studies do not agree on what rent limits do. Research in Catalonia found that rents were 4 to 6% lower with limits ([Jofre-Monseny et al., 2023](https://diposit.ub.edu/bitstreams/ef7e8e25-4e85-44d5-af74-8006deafeae2/download); [Monras and Montalvo, 2023](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp2023-28.pdf)). The studies disagree on whether fewer homes were offered for rent. After the 2024 limits, the number of rental ads fell by about 20%, according to the Fedea think tank ([Fedea](https://documentos.fedea.net/pubs/ap/2026/ap2026-15.pdf)).

On buying, several parties offer help to buyers. PP offers 4% VAT on a first home for people under 40 and a state guarantee for the deposit. PSOE offers loans of up to €50,000 with no interest, and Junts offers tax deductions for mortgages and rent. Most of these are realistic. But Spain finishes only about 100,000 homes a year ([Housing Ministry](https://www.lamoncloa.gob.es/serviciosdeprensa/notasprensa/vivienda-agenda-urbana/Documents/2025/040725-ObservatoriodeviviendaysueloBoletnanual24.pdf)), so in our view part of any help for buyers ends up in higher prices. Podemos and Sumar want to ban buying a home you will not live in (Sumar would still allow buying to rent at a controlled price). EH Bildu, Junts and Adelante Andalucía want to ban companies, funds or banks from buying homes. We rate these not possible, because EU law protects the free movement of money. Coalición Canaria wants to limit home buying by foreigners on the islands, but this cannot apply to EU citizens.

Many parties, from PNV to Podemos, want to close a gap in the law on short-term rentals (*alquiler de temporada*). Some landlords use these contracts to avoid the rules that protect tenants. Closing the gap is a simple change to the Urban Leases Law (*LAU*). If you rent short-term or rent out a flat, watch for this change. [See all housing promises](?topic=housing#explorer-app).

### Taxes, including the Beckham regime

The special tax regime for foreign workers who move to Spain (*régimen de trabajadores desplazados*, often called the "Beckham law") would end under the Podemos, Sumar and ERC programmes. This needs only an ordinary law, so we rate it realistic. But these parties would need other parties' votes to get a majority in Congress. On the other side, **PP** and **Junts** want to raise the income tax (*IRPF*) bands each year with inflation. PP also offers young workers aged 18 to 35 a lower income tax for four years.

On the left, PSOE and ERC want a 35% top rate on large income from savings and investments. Sumar wants the tax on large fortunes to start at €1 million instead of €3 million. Junts wants to end inheritance tax, which the regions mostly control. PP and PNV promise 0% VAT on basic food again. When Spain did this in 2023, shop prices fell by almost the full amount of the cut ([Banco de España](https://www.bde.es/wbe/en/publicaciones/analisis-economico-investigacion/documentos-trabajo/analysing-the-vat-cut-pass-through-in-spain-using-web-scraped-supermarket-data-and-machine-learning.html)). But half of the savings went to the richest 40% of households, according to Fedea ([Fedea](https://documentos.fedea.net/pubs/ap/2023/ap2023-02.pdf)). [See all tax promises](?topic=taxes#explorer-app), or use our [net salary calculator](/calculators/net-salary) to see how today's rules affect your pay.

### Immigration, *arraigo* and residence

*Arraigo* is a way to get residence based on your ties to Spain, such as years lived here, work or family. The parties disagree on it more than on anything else. **Vox** would end it. **PP** would keep it only for special cases decided one by one, and would ban mass regularisations. **Sumar, Podemos and EH Bildu** want a permanent way for people without papers to get legal status, and **Adelante Andalucía** asks for a new extraordinary regularisation. Most *arraigo* rules are in the immigration regulation (*Reglamento de Extranjería*), so a government can change them by royal decree. The special regularisation of 2026 was done this way ([BOE](https://www.boe.es/buscar/doc.php?id=BOE-A-2026-8284)). Changes to the Immigration Law itself (*Ley de Extranjería*), such as PP's ban on mass regularisations, need an organic law, so 176 votes. The 2026 regularisation is already over. It received 1,174,978 applications ([La Moncloa](https://www.lamoncloa.gob.es/serviciosdeprensa/notasprensa/inclusion/Paginas/2026/020726-balance-regularizacion-extraordinaria.aspx)). The debate now is about what comes next.

Some promises put Spanish citizens before foreigners who live here legally. Examples are Vox's "national priority" (*prioridad nacional*) for housing and benefits, and its tax on money sent abroad. We rate these not possible, because they go against the equality article of the Constitution and against EU law. There are also practical promises. Sumar wants easier registration at the town hall (*empadronamiento*) and faster recognition of foreign degrees. Vox and PSOE both promise to end compulsory online appointments (*cita previa*) at state offices. [See all immigration promises](?topic=immigration#explorer-app).

### Work and self-employment

If you are self-employed (*autónomo*), PP and Vox offer no social security fees for people who are new or earn little. PSOE would continue the slow move to fees based on real income, and Adelante Andalucía would go further, with a zero fee when there is no income. The parties disagree most on a shorter working week. PSOE and Sumar want 37.5 hours, BNG wants 35, EH Bildu and Adelante Andalucía want 32 (Sumar's long-term goal too), and Podemos wants 30. In September 2025 Congress already rejected a bill for 37.5 hours and sent it back to the government ([Congress](https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L15/CONG/BOCG/A/BOCG-15-A-58-3.PDF)), so we rate all of these unlikely. Vox would cancel the 2021 labour reform. Since that reform, the share of temporary contracts has fallen from 25.4% to 15.1% ([INE](https://www.ine.es/daco/daco42/daco4211/epa0421.pdf); [Labour Ministry](https://www.mites.gob.es/ficheros/ministerio/sec_trabajo/analisis_mercado_trabajo/EPA/2025/Cuarto_trimestre/EPA-2025-T4-MITES-SGEAS.pdf)). [See all work promises](?topic=labour#explorer-app).

### Pensions, health and energy bills

Almost every pension promise costs more money. Some parties want a lower retirement age: 63 for Podemos, 64 for Adelante Andalucía, and 65 for everyone again for Sumar and BNG. We rate these unlikely because of the cost. On health, nearly every party promises more money. In our view the real problem is the lack of doctors and nurses, and the regions run the health service. On energy, some parties want to keep the nuclear plants open longer (PP, Vox, Junts), and others want to keep the plan to close them by 2035 (PSOE, Sumar, Podemos, EH Bildu). Nuclear power produced 19% of Spain's electricity in 2025 ([REE](https://www.ree.es/en/press-office/news/press-release/2026/03/the-spanish-electricity-system-in-2025-electricity-demand-generation-and-installed-power-capacity-increase)), and the Almaraz plant can already stay open until June 2030 ([BOE](https://www.boe.es/boe/dias/2026/08/14/pdfs/BOE-A-2026-17756.pdf)). See the promises on [pensions](?topic=pensions#explorer-app), [health](?topic=health#explorer-app) and [energy](?topic=energy#explorer-app).

## The bigger picture

Whoever wins, five limits decide what a government can do:

- **Changing the Constitution needs PP and PSOE together.** A change needs three-fifths of both chambers ([art. 167](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a167)). Changes to basic rights or to the Crown need two-thirds, a new election and a referendum ([art. 168](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a168)).
- **Organic laws need 176 votes** ([art. 81](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a81)). They include the judges' council, the Immigration Law, the education law, the "gag law" and the Criminal Code. The group of parties that reaches 176 seats decides them.
- **The regions** run housing, health, education and land use. The central government can often only give money and ask them to act.
- **EU law** limits VAT rates, trade deals, climate goals, the EU Migration Pact and any different treatment by nationality.
- **Money.** Spain is using an extended 2023 budget ([Finance Ministry](https://www.hacienda.gob.es/es-ES/Prensa/Noticias/Paginas/2025/20251223-NP-CM-PRORROGA-PGE.aspx)), and EU rules limit how much the state can spend. In almost every programme, the promises cost more than the planned income.

The realistic promises often bring the same pros and cons. Help for buyers and renters works fastest. In our view, though, part of it turns into higher prices while there are too few homes. Tax cuts from the right give people more money now but make the deficit bigger. New taxes from the left ask more from rich people and large companies. In our view they bring in less than planned, because money can move to other countries. Stricter immigration rules send a signal that entering without papers does not lead to legal status. But they leave more people working without contracts. A permanent regularisation does the opposite. It may attract more people to come, but experts disagree on how many. Giving more powers to one region brings government closer to the people there, and then other regions ask for the same.

## FAQ

### When is Spain's 2026 general election?

On Sunday, 29 November 2026. It is an early election for both chambers of parliament, the Congress of Deputies and the Senate. After it, Congress votes on a prime minister ([art. 99](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a99)). In the first vote the candidate needs an absolute majority, 176 of 350. In a second vote more yes votes than no votes are enough.

### Can foreigners vote in the Spanish general election?

No. Only Spanish citizens can vote in general elections ([art. 13 of the Constitution](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-1978-31229#a13)). EU citizens who are registered in Spain can vote in local and European elections. Citizens of some other countries can vote in local elections if their country has an agreement with Spain (see [who can vote](/articles/how-spain-votes)).

### Which parties want to end the Beckham law?

Podemos, Sumar and ERC want to end the special tax regime for foreign workers who move to Spain. We rate it realistic, because an ordinary law is enough. But it needs a majority in Congress, and none of these parties has one alone.

### What does "realistic" mean in this guide?

A realistic promise does not go against the Constitution or EU law. A government can carry it out with an ordinary law, a government decision or an affordable amount of money (A). Some realistic promises are harder because they need an organic law, the regions' agreement, a lot of money or several years (B). The rating does not say if a policy is good or bad, and it does not predict that a promise will be kept.

### Will the rent limits in Catalonia end?

PP and Vox promise to cancel the Housing Law that allows them. Together they would need only an ordinary law. Other parties promise to keep the limits or make them stricter. The result depends on which group of parties can form a majority after 29 November.
