You've gone freelance in Spain, maybe on the digital nomad visa. You're an *autónomo* now. So how much of every euro do you actually keep?

Here's the short answer. A freelance designer in Madrid who invoices **€40,000** in 2026 and spends **€8,000** on the business keeps about **€22,400**. Around **€5,100** goes to Social Security and **€4,450** to income tax. This guide shows where every one of those euros goes and why, with the 2026 figures: the Social Security brackets, how income tax (IRPF) is worked out, IVA, what you can deduct, the forms and the dates. You can run your own numbers in our free [autónomo tax calculator](/calculators/autonomo-tax) as you read.

## In short: what an autónomo pays

- **Social Security (cuota de autónomos)** A monthly payment of €205.88 to €607.35 in 2026, depending on your profit, or a flat €80 (about €88.65 with the MEI levy) in your first year with the tarifa plana.
- **Income tax (IRPF)** Charged on your profit after expenses and Social Security, at progressive rates of about 19% to 47%. Half goes to the state, half to your region. You prepay it during the year through withholding on invoices (15%, or 7% when you start) or quarterly with form 130, and settle it in the annual return, the Renta.
- **IVA (VAT)** Usually 21% added to your invoices. It isn't your money: you collect it, subtract the IVA on your business purchases and pay the difference every quarter with form 303.
- **What's left** For a typical freelancer, Social Security and IRPF together take about 25-35% of the profit. On €32,000 of profit in Madrid it's 30%.

## Who is an autónomo

An autónomo is anyone in Spain who works for themselves, regularly, as a person and not through a company. Freelance designers, developers, translators, consultants, plumbers, shop owners, Airbnb hosts offering hotel-like services, online sellers: all autónomos. Spanish law calls it the *Régimen Especial de Trabajadores Autónomos*, or **RETA**, which is the Social Security scheme you join.

Two registrations make you one:

- **Hacienda (the tax agency)** You file form **036** to tell the tax agency what you do (your activity code in the IAE, the business activity tax), when you start and which taxes you'll file. Form 037, the simplified version, was withdrawn in 2025, so everyone now uses the 036.
- **Social Security** You register in the RETA through the Import@ss portal, up to 60 days before you start and no later than the day you start. You pick your expected income bracket here.

**Digital nomad visa.** If you're on the visa as a freelancer working for clients (foreign or, up to 20% of your work, Spanish), you register as an autónomo and everything in this guide applies to you. If you're on the same visa as a remote *employee* of a foreign company, you're not an autónomo: you're an employee and pay through a different route. The special "Beckham law" tax regime for people who move to Spain is mainly for employees and only open to some self-employed people, so get advice before you count on it.

**IAE.** You'll be registered in the business activity tax, the *Impuesto sobre Actividades Económicas*, but as an individual you don't pay it. Only companies with a turnover above €1 million do.

Being autónomo is not a choice in the sense of a tax option. If you work for yourself regularly, you must register. There's an old myth that you only need to register once you earn more than the minimum wage; the courts have accepted it in a few cases of truly occasional work, but Social Security and Hacienda don't, and fines for working unregistered are real.

## The three bills: Social Security, IRPF, IVA

As an autónomo you deal with three different things, and they work in very different ways.

- **Social Security** It's a monthly payment, charged by direct debit at the end of each month. It covers your public pension, sick pay, parental leave and the self-employed unemployment benefit. Since 2023 it depends on your profit.
- **IRPF** The *Impuesto sobre la Renta de las Personas Físicas* is the same personal income tax employees pay. You pay it on your profit: partly during the year, and the rest in your annual return.
- **IVA** The *Impuesto sobre el Valor Añadido* is Spain's VAT. It isn't really your money. You collect it from clients and pass it on to the tax agency.

The first two are real costs and come out of your profit. The third mostly passes through you. That's why the calculator and most of this guide talk about profit before IVA.

## Everything starts from your profit

Almost everything starts from one number: your profit for the year, the *rendimiento neto*.

- **Income** Everything you invoiced in the year, without the IVA.
- **Minus expenses** Your deductible business expenses.
- **Equals profit** What's left is your net profit.

Social Security and income tax are both worked out from it, each in its own way. Social Security uses it to put you in a bracket. Income tax uses it as the start of your taxable income.

So for our designer: €40,000 invoiced minus €8,000 of expenses is a profit of **€32,000**. Keep that number in mind; we'll follow it all the way through.

Most autónomos use the simplified direct method, *estimación directa simplificada*: real income minus real expenses, with a few simplifications. If your turnover goes above €600,000 you move to the normal direct method. Some small trades (taxis, some farming and retail) can use *módulos*, a fixed system based on indicators like floor space or number of vehicles. That's a different world, and it isn't covered here.

## Social Security: the 15 brackets

Since 2023, what you pay to Social Security depends on what you earn. There are **15 brackets**, the *tramos*, based on your average monthly net profit.

Before matching you to a bracket, Social Security takes **7% off your profit** for general expenses (3% if you're a company director, an *autónomo societario*). So the number that counts is your annual profit, minus 7%, divided by 12.

::: graphic ss-brackets

In 2026, the minimum monthly quota runs from **€205.88** in the lowest bracket to **€607.35** at the top, above €6,000 a month. Each figure is the minimum contribution base for the bracket times 31.50%: 28.30% for common contingencies, 1.30% for professional ones, 0.90% for the cese de actividad, 0.10% for training and 0.90% for the MEI, an intergenerational equity levy that funds pensions.

Our designer: €32,000 minus 7% is €29,760, or €2,480 a month. That's **bracket 10: €427.21 a month, €5,126.52 a year**.

At the bottom of the scale the quota is heavy: €206 a month can take almost a third of a very small income. At the top it's around a tenth. Two things soften it:

- **It's fully deductible** The whole quota comes off your income for IRPF. At a 30% marginal rate, €427 a month really costs you about €300.
- **It builds your pension** Within your bracket you can choose a higher base and pay more. That raises your future pension and your sick pay, at a higher cost now. Many autónomos stay on the minimum and save privately instead.

Until 2023 most autónomos simply paid the minimum, whatever they earned, and plenty of older advice online still assumes you can. You can't any more: the bracket follows your real profit.

## The yearly regularización

Here's the catch. Social Security can't know your profit in advance.

So you **pick a bracket yourself**, based on what you expect to earn that year. You can change it up to **six times a year**, and each change takes effect on the first day of the next odd month: 1 March, 1 May, 1 July, 1 September, 1 November and 1 January. If your income drops mid-year, move down; if a big contract comes in, move up.

Then, after you file your annual tax return, the tax agency tells Social Security what you really earned. Social Security compares it with the bracket you paid:

- **Paid too much** You get the difference back, without having to ask.
- **Paid too little** You get a bill for the rest, payable by the end of the month after you're notified.

::: graphic regularizacion

This settlement is the *regularización*. It usually comes about a year after the year ends, sometimes later: the first ones, for 2023, only arrived between December 2024 and spring 2025. Notifications come electronically, so check your Social Security inbox and the DEHú, the government's notifications site.

Practical tip: if you're unsure, pick a bracket that matches your realistic profit and keep a little aside. A surprise bill a year later is the most common shock new autónomos get.

## Income tax: from profit to taxable income

Now, income tax: the IRPF. How do you get from profit to the amount that's actually taxed?

- **Income minus expenses** Here the Social Security quota counts as an expense too, all of it.
- **Minus 5% for hard-to-prove expenses** Under the simplified direct method you get this allowance on top of your real expenses: 5% of the profit after Social Security, up to €2,000 a year. You don't need receipts for it.
- **Equals your taxable business income** It goes into your annual return together with any other income you have: a salary, rent from a flat, interest.

Our designer: €32,000 minus €5,126.52 of Social Security is €26,873.48. Five percent of that is €1,343.67, which leaves **€25,529.81 of taxable income**.

::: graphic profit-path

**The 20% start-up reduction.** In your first tax year with a profit and in the year after, you can usually take another **20% off** your net business income, on up to €100,000 of it (art. 32.3 of the IRPF law). It applies even to a short first year, if you started in April, say. The conditions: you didn't run a business in the previous year, and it doesn't apply if more than half of your business income that year comes from a company you worked for as an employee the year before. It's easy to miss and worth hundreds or thousands of euros, so check your gestor has claimed it. The calculator doesn't include it, so its figures for a new autónomo are on the safe side.

## IRPF brackets and the personal allowance

IRPF is progressive. Your income is cut into slices, and each slice is taxed at its own rate. Half of each rate is set by the state and half by your region; on the typical scale, both halves together give:

- **Up to €12,450** About 19%
- **€12,450 – €20,200** About 24%
- **€20,200 – €35,200** About 30%
- **€35,200 – €60,000** About 37%
- **€60,000 – €300,000** About 45%
- **Above €300,000** About 47%

Then comes the personal allowance, the *mínimo personal*: **€5,550** of income that's effectively tax-free (some regions set a higher figure for their half; Madrid uses €5,956.65). It isn't subtracted from your income: the tax on the allowance is subtracted from the tax on your income, which works out at about €1,050 less tax on the typical scale.

The allowance grows with your family and situation. The state figures:

- **Children** €2,400 for the first, €2,700 for the second, €4,000 for the third, €4,500 for each one after that, plus €2,800 for each child under 3.
- **Your age** €1,150 more from 65, another €1,400 from 75.
- **Parents living with you** €1,150 for each parent over 65 who lives with you and earns under €8,000 a year, plus €1,400 if they're over 75.
- **Disability** €3,000 at 33% or more (€6,000 if you need help to get around or have reduced mobility), and €12,000 at 65% or more.

With two children, one of them under 3, our designer's IRPF in Madrid drops from €4,453 to about **€2,934**.

## Myth: a higher bracket taxes all your income

A very common fear: you earn a bit more, cross into the next bracket, and suddenly all your income is taxed at the higher rate. That's not how it works.

Take a taxable income of €25,500 on the typical scale. Only the euros inside each slice pay that slice's rate:

::: graphic slices

So about €5,815 in total, before the personal allowance. Thirty percent of everything would have been €7,650.

The useful number is your **marginal rate**, the rate on your next euro. At this level it's 30%: a thousand euros more profit means €300 more tax, and the other €700 stay yours. Add Social Security, which may move you up a bracket, and it's still far from the "it's not worth earning more" that you sometimes hear.

## Why your region matters

Here's something unusual about Spain. Half of your IRPF goes to the state and half to your region (the *comunidad autónoma*), and each region sets its own brackets, rates and allowances for its half.

So the same profit is taxed differently in Madrid, Andalusia, Valencia or Catalonia, and the gap grows the more you earn. Madrid has the lowest regional rates of the common-system regions; Catalonia and Valencia are among the highest at middle and upper incomes.

::: graphic regions

Three places are different again:

- **Navarre and the Basque Country** Their own separate tax systems (*régimen foral*), with their own tax agencies, rules and rates. Social Security is the same as everywhere else.
- **Ceuta and Melilla** Residents get 60% off the income tax on income they earn there.
- **The Canary Islands** The normal IRPF, with their own regional half, but no IVA (more on that below).

It's the region where you live most of the year that counts, not where your clients are. You can compare regions for your own profit in the [autónomo tax calculator](/calculators/autonomo-tax): change the region and watch the regional half move. Or see every region side by side for [€40,000 of net revenue](/calculators/autonomo-tax-40000-euros).

## Worked example: €32,000 in Madrid, step by step

Let's put it all together, the way the calculator does. A freelance designer in Madrid: €40,000 invoiced, €8,000 in expenses. Single, no children, an autónomo for more than two years, so no tarifa plana.

- **Annual net revenue** €40,000 − €8,000 = €32,000
- **Profit for the Social Security bracket** €32,000 − 7% = €29,760, or €2,480 a month
- **Social Security** Bracket 10: €427.21 × 12 = €5,126.52
- **Profit after Social Security** €32,000 − €5,126.52 = €26,873.48
- **5% hard-to-prove expenses** €1,343.67 (under the €2,000 cap)
- **Taxable income** €25,529.81
- **State half of IRPF** €2,384.97
- **Madrid half of IRPF** €2,068.43
- **Total IRPF** €4,453.40
- **Kept for the year** €22,420.08, about €1,868 a month

About 30% of the profit went to Social Security and IRPF, and 14% of it to IRPF alone. That 14% is the *effective* rate, very different from the 30% marginal rate.

## Paying during the year: withholding and form 130

You don't wait until the end of the year to pay your income tax. You pay it in advance, in one of two ways.

### Withholding on your invoices (retención)

If you're a **professional**, say a designer, consultant, developer, translator, architect or lawyer, and you invoice **Spanish businesses or other autónomos**, they keep part of each invoice and pay it to the tax agency for you. That's the *retención*.

- **The standard rate is 15%.**
- **New professionals: 7%.** In the year you start and the next two, if you had no professional activity in the year before you started. Tell your clients in writing that you qualify.

On a €1,000 invoice with 21% IVA and 15% withholding, the client pays €1,000 + €210 − €150 = **€1,060**. The €150 is an advance on your own IRPF; the client pays it to Hacienda in your name and you get credit for it in your Renta.

::: graphic invoice

Private clients and foreign clients don't withhold. Most trades that aren't "professional" activities in the tax sense (a shop, a plumber, a hairdresser) don't have withholding either.

### Form 130: quarterly prepayment

Without withholding, you prepay income tax yourself every quarter, with **form 130**: **20% of your profit so far this year**, minus what you've already paid in earlier quarters and minus any withholding you've had. The quarters are cumulative, so a bad quarter after a good one can bring the payment to zero.

If **at least 70%** of last year's business income already had withholding, you don't file the 130 at all. In your first year, it's this year's income that counts. Many freelancers who work only for Spanish companies never file one.

Freelancers who work mostly for **clients abroad**, which includes most people on the digital nomad visa, have no withholding, so they file the 130 every quarter.

Either way, these are only advance payments. The final bill comes in the annual return.

## The Renta settles it

The Renta, the annual income tax return, is where everything gets settled. You file it between early April and **30 June** of the following year, online, with the tax agency's Renta Web. Unlike many employees, **almost every autónomo has to file the Renta**, whatever their profit: the income thresholds that let many employees skip it don't apply to business income (only total income under €1,000 a year is exempt).

Say all your clients are Spanish businesses and you invoice €40,000 in a year. Fifteen percent is kept back: **€6,000**. But your real income tax for the year comes to only about €4,450. So the Renta gives you about **€1,550 back**.

With no withholding, the quarterly form 130 payments do the same job, and the Renta settles the difference. At 20% of profit, the 130 often overshoots a little at middle incomes, so a refund is common too.

**If you owe money**, you can split it: **60% in June** and **40% in early November**, at no extra cost. Choose it when you file.

In the Renta you also claim things the quarterly forms don't see: the personal and family allowances, pension plan contributions, regional deductions (for rent, children, gym fees and many more, depending on the region), and the 20% start-up reduction.

Don't simply accept the draft (*borrador*) the tax agency prepares. It doesn't know your business income and expenses; you, or your gestor, add them.

Your Renta matters beyond tax, too. When you ask a Spanish bank for a mortgage, it judges you on the income in your returns, not your turnover: see [getting a mortgage as an autónomo](/articles/spain-property-guide#getting-a-mortgage) in our guide to buying property in Spain.

## A private pension plan: up to €5,750 off your taxable income

One of the best tax breaks an autónomo has: a private pension plan. You can put in up to **€5,750 a year** and take all of it off your taxable income.

- **€1,500** In any personal pension plan (*plan de pensiones individual*).
- **€4,250 more** In a plan made for the self-employed, the *plan de pensiones de empleo simplificado* (PPES), offered by banks and fund managers since 2023.

There's one cap: the reduction can't be more than **30% of your net income from work and business**. On a €15,000 profit that limits you to about €3,400.

You save at your top tax rate. For our designer in Madrid, with €25,530 of taxable income, putting in the full €5,750 cuts IRPF by about **€1,590**. At €60,000 of profit, where the marginal rate is about 36%, it saves about **€2,060** a year.

::: graphic pension

Two things to know before you do it:

- **You pay tax when you take it out.** The whole amount, contributions and growth, is taxed as ordinary income in the year you receive it. But by then you're usually retired, with little other income, so you land in a lower bracket. Taking it as a monthly income rather than a lump sum keeps the rate down.
- **The money is locked in.** You can take it out when you retire, after ten years in the plan (for contributions made from 2015), or earlier only in special cases: serious illness, long-term unemployment, severe dependency, or the end of your business activity for autónomos.

Compare fees: the cheapest index-linked plans charge well under 0.5% a year, while some bank plans charge 1.5% or more, which eats much of the tax saving over time.

## IVA: who really pays it

Now the third bill: IVA, Spain's VAT. And the big question is, who actually pays it?

On most goods and services you add **21%** to your invoice. Some things carry **10%** (restaurants, passenger transport, some housing) or **4%** (bread, books, medicines).

Say you invoice €1,000 for a design job. Your client pays €1,210. Those €210 aren't yours.

But you can subtract the IVA you paid on your own business purchases. Buy a €1,000 laptop for the business and you pay €210 of IVA on it, which you get back by subtracting it from the IVA you collected.

Every quarter you file **form 303** and pay the difference. If you paid more IVA than you charged, the difference carries over to the next quarter, or comes back at the end of the year (in the last quarter you choose a refund). In January you also file **form 390**, the annual IVA summary.

::: graphic iva-quarter

So who pays? The final consumer. A business client deducts the IVA again in its own 303, and you're only the collector. But for private clients it's part of the price: a €1,000 job costs them €1,210. So IVA shapes what you can charge private clients, even though it never touches your profit.

## IVA exceptions: EU clients, clients abroad, the Canaries

There are exceptions worth knowing, and freelancers with foreign clients meet most of them.

- **Exempt activities** Healthcare, regulated education, insurance and financial services, among others. You charge no IVA, but you can't deduct the IVA on your purchases either, so it becomes a cost.
- **Business clients in another EU country** No Spanish IVA. The client accounts for it in its own country, the *reverse charge* (*inversión del sujeto pasivo*). You need to be in the EU VAT register (the ROI, which you ask for in form 036), check the client's VAT number in VIES, write "reverse charge" on the invoice, and file **form 349** for those sales.
- **Clients outside the EU** Services to businesses outside the EU usually carry no Spanish IVA. For private clients outside the EU it depends on the service; consulting, advertising, IT and translation usually go without.
- **Digital services to private people across the EU** Above **€10,000 a year** of such sales to other EU countries, you charge each customer's own VAT rate, through the EU one-stop shop, the **OSS**, instead of filing in every country.
- **The Canary Islands** No IVA at all, but a local tax, the **IGIC**, with a general rate of **7%**, filed with the Canary tax agency. **Ceuta and Melilla** have their own, the **IPSI**.
- **Shops selling to the public** Usually a special scheme, the *recargo de equivalencia*: suppliers add an extra surcharge on their invoices and the shopkeeper files no IVA returns at all.

One change to watch: since 2025, EU rules let countries free small businesses with turnover under **€85,000** a year from charging VAT. As of November 2026 Spain hasn't adopted it. If it does, this part will change for many small freelancers.

## What you can deduct

Now the part that lowers all of these: your expenses. They reduce your profit, so they reduce both income tax and, through your bracket, Social Security. What counts as deductible? Three rules:

- **Linked to your business** Directly, and only in proportion if you also use it privately.
- **A proper invoice in your name** A *factura* with your tax ID (NIF or NIE).
- **Recorded in your books** The income and expense ledgers every autónomo keeps.

The usual ones:

- **Your Social Security quota** All of it, every month.
- **Your gestor or accountant** And legal fees, bank fees on the business account.
- **Software and subscriptions** Design tools, hosting, cloud storage, your invoicing app.
- **Coworking or an office** Rent, plus its utilities.
- **Marketing** Ads, your website, business cards.
- **Training** Courses and books related to your work.
- **Phone and internet** The share you use for work.
- **Private health insurance** Up to **€500 a year for each person covered**: you, your spouse, and each child under 25 who lives with you (€1,500 for a person with a disability). A married couple with two kids can deduct up to €2,000.

**Equipment.** A laptop or other equipment isn't deducted all at once. It's spread over several years as depreciation, the *amortización*: computers can be written off at up to 26% a year, so in about four years. Small items, up to about **€300 each**, can usually be written off in one go (up to €25,000 a year in total). The IVA on them, though, you deduct in full straight away.

What you can't deduct: fines, gifts beyond small amounts, your own salary, clothes you could wear outside work, and anything without an invoice in your name.

## Home office, meals and the car

Three things cause the most confusion.

### Working from home

If you work from home, you can deduct part of the rent or mortgage interest, the IBI (property tax), the community fees and home insurance, **in proportion to the space you use for work**. Declare that share to the tax agency (in form 036) first: if you use a 15 m² room in a 75 m² flat, that's 20%.

Utilities are stricter: water, electricity, gas and internet at home count only **30% of that work share**. With a 20% office, it's 30% of 20%: **6%** of the bill. (The internet line may be argued at a higher share if it's clearly used for work; your gestor will know what holds up.) Cutting the bill itself is worth it too: here's [how switching electricity provider works](/articles/switching-electricity-provider-spain).

::: graphic home-office

If you rent, the rent counts in the same proportion, but your landlord may need to know: some leases forbid business use.

### Meals: the dietas

Meals on business trips, the *dietas*, are deductible but capped: about **€26.67 a day in Spain** and **€48.08 abroad**, or €53.34 and €91.35 if the trip includes an overnight stay. Conditions: paid by card or other electronic means, in a restaurant or hotel, and clearly linked to your work. The tax agency looks hardest at meals in your own town, so many gestores only claim dietas away from it; a lunch with a client in your own city is usually treated not as a dieta, though some gestores deduct it as client entertainment, up to 1% of turnover.

### The car

For income tax, a car is deductible only if it's **used exclusively for work**. That's hard to prove unless you're, say, a sales rep, a driving instructor or a taxi driver. A car you also use at weekends doesn't qualify, nor do its fuel, insurance or parking, although parking and tolls on specific work trips usually do.

IVA is more generous: the tax agency presumes a car is **50% used for the business**, so you can usually deduct half the IVA on it and its running costs.

## Invoices and records

Every invoice you send, and every one you collect, matters.

A proper *factura* has:

- a number in an unbroken sequence (you can use a separate series for corrections);
- the date;
- both sides' names, tax IDs and addresses;
- a description of what was sold;
- the amount, with the IVA rate and amount, and any withholding, shown separately.

A plain till receipt (*ticket*, or simplified invoice) usually isn't enough. Ask for a full invoice with your tax ID on it; any shop has to give you one if you ask.

Remember that IRPF and IVA are separate checks. €1,000 of software plus €210 of IVA lowers your profit by €1,000 and your IVA bill by €210, not your profit by €1,210.

Keep everything (invoices, receipts, bank statements) for **at least six years**, and longer for anything you're still depreciating. The tax agency can usually look back four years, Social Security four, and commercial law asks for six.

Spain is also moving to invoicing software that meets new anti-fraud rules, **VeriFactu**: every invoice is registered with a tamper-proof record and, optionally, sent to the tax agency in real time. It was due to become compulsory in 2027 (1 January for companies, 1 July for autónomos), but in October 2026 the government announced another delay, to October 2028, to line it up with compulsory electronic invoicing between businesses: from October 2027 for companies invoicing over €8 million, and from October 2028 for autónomos and smaller firms. Check the final dates when you choose your invoicing software; most paid tools for autónomos already support VeriFactu.

## The tarifa plana for new autónomos

If you're new, there's a discount: the *tarifa plana*.

- **First 12 months** You pay a flat **€80 a month**, about **€88.65** with the MEI levy, whatever you earn.
- **Next 12 months** If your net profit stays below the minimum wage (€17,094 a year in 2026), you can ask to extend it for another 12 months.
- **Who qualifies** Anyone registering for the first time, or who hasn't been an autónomo in the last two years (three if you had the tarifa plana before).

The €80 was set in law for 2023-2025; for 2026 it has carried over while the new figure waits for a state budget, so check the current amount when you register.

Some regions go further and pay that quota back to you, in full, for the first year or two: Madrid, Andalusia and Murcia have had such schemes. Check what your region offers; most require you to apply within a set time after registering.

Even with the tarifa plana, the regularización still happens at the end, but only for the months after the flat rate ends. The [autónomo tax calculator](/calculators/autonomo-tax) has a first-year switch, so you can see what the tarifa plana is worth at your income.

## Company directors: the autónomo societario

If you run your business through your own company, usually a *sociedad limitada* (SL), and you control it or work in it as a director, you're an *autónomo societario*. It changes three things:

- **A higher minimum quota** At least about **€449 a month** in 2026 (a minimum base of €1,424.40), whatever the bracket table says.
- **A smaller allowance** Social Security takes only **3%** off for general expenses, not 7%.
- **Different tax** The company pays corporate tax (*impuesto sobre sociedades*, 23-25%, lower for small and new companies) on its profit, and you pay IRPF on the salary and dividends it pays you.

A company can pay off at higher profits, when you can leave part of the money in it, but it costs more to run (accounts, a notary, separate filings). That's a topic for another guide.

## Second jobs, family, stopping

A few situations change the picture.

- **Employed and self-employed at the same time** That's *pluriactividad*. You still pay the full autónomo quota on your freelance profit, but if your common-contingency contributions as employee and autónomo together go over €17,323.68 in 2026, Social Security refunds half of the excess (up to half of your autónomo quotas for those contingencies) the following year. For IRPF the two incomes simply add up, and you'll usually owe tax in the Renta, because your employer withheld as if the salary were all you had. The [net salary calculator](/calculators/net-salary) shows what that salary nets on its own.
- **A family member who works with you** A partner or child can join as an *autónomo colaborador* (details below).
- **Stopping** You deregister with Social Security within three days of stopping and with Hacienda (form 036) within a month. You still file the last quarter and the Renta for that year.
- **Unemployment benefit for the self-employed** Your quotas give you a right to the *cese de actividad* if you stop for economic, technical or other set reasons and meet the conditions, mainly 12 months of continuous contributions just before. It pays 70% of your average base for 4 to 24 months, depending on how long you've contributed.
- **Sick leave and parental leave** Your quota also covers sick pay (from the fourth day off work) and 19 weeks of parental leave. During longer sick leave, from the 61st day, Social Security pays your quota too.

## The autónomo colaborador, in detail

Let's look closer at one of these: the *autónomo colaborador*.

It's a family member who works in your business regularly: your spouse, or a relative up to the second degree (a child, a parent, a sibling, a grandchild or grandparent, including in-laws) who lives with you and depends on you.

- **Not an employee** They register in the self-employed scheme themselves and have their own quota: at least about **€449 a month**, the same minimum as a company director.
- **A discount to start** A new colaborador who hasn't been self-employed in the last five years gets 50% off for the first 18 months, then 25% for six more.
- **You can pay them** For a spouse or minor child with a work contract, the pay is a deductible expense for you, as long as the work is real and the pay is in line with the market. For other relatives, ask your gestor first.
- **Withholding** On that salary you keep back IRPF, like an employer, and pay it with form 111, with the annual summary in form 190.
- **Their taxes** The colaborador files no quarterly returns of their own. They include that pay in their own annual Renta.

Splitting income like this can lower the family's total tax, because each person starts again at the bottom of the scale and gets their own personal allowance. But the work has to be real, and the extra quota has to be worth it: it usually pays off only when your own profit is well into the 37% or 45% bands.

## The tax calendar

Let's put it all on a calendar.

::: graphic calendar

- **Every month** The Social Security quota, charged at the end of the month.
- **Every quarter** By the 20th of April, July and October, and by 30 January: the IVA return (form 303) and the income tax prepayment (form 130, if you need it). If you have employees, pay other professionals with withholding or rent an office, there are a few more: forms 111 and 115. EU sales go in form 349.
- **In January** The annual summaries: form 390 for IVA, and 190 and 180 if you filed 111 or 115.
- **In February** Form 347, for anyone you dealt with for more than €3,005.06 in the year.
- **April to 30 June** The annual income tax return, the Renta.
- **Some time after that** The Social Security regularización for the year.

Missing a deadline without being asked costs a surcharge of 1% plus 1% per full month late, from 1% up to 15% after a year, plus interest beyond that. If the tax agency asks first, the fines are much bigger. Set up reminders, or let a gestor handle it: a monthly fee of €40-80 is common for a simple freelance business, and it's deductible.

## Summary

- Everything starts from your profit: what you invoiced without IVA, minus deductible expenses.
- Social Security: 15 brackets on your profit minus 7%, €205.88 to €607.35 a month in 2026, settled a year later in the regularización. €80 a month in your first year with the tarifa plana.
- IRPF: profit minus Social Security, minus 5% (max €2,000), minus 20% in your first two profitable years, taxed at about 19-47% with the personal allowance on top. Half state, half region.
- You prepay IRPF by withholding (15%, or 7% when new) or with form 130 (20% of profit), and settle it in the Renta by 30 June.
- A pension plan can take up to €5,750 a year off your taxable income.
- IVA is collected, not earned: 21% on most invoices, form 303 every quarter, with exceptions for EU and foreign clients and the Canaries.
- Keep proper invoices for every expense and every sale for six years.

Try your own numbers, for your region and family, in the free [autónomo tax calculator](/calculators/autonomo-tax). And if you're comparing freelancing with a job, the [net salary calculator](/calculators/net-salary) shows the same steps for an employee.

## FAQ

### How much tax does an autónomo pay in Spain?

Social Security plus IRPF usually take about 25-35% of a freelancer's profit. A freelancer in Madrid with €32,000 of profit (€40,000 invoiced, €8,000 in expenses) pays €5,127 in Social Security and €4,453 in IRPF in 2026 and keeps about €22,420, or 70% of the profit. IVA comes on top but is paid by clients. Work out your own figure with the [autónomo tax calculator](/calculators/autonomo-tax).

### How much is the autónomo Social Security quota in 2026?

Between €205.88 and €607.35 a month, depending on your average monthly net profit after a 7% allowance, in 15 brackets. With €2,480 a month you're in bracket 10 and pay €427.21. New autónomos with the tarifa plana pay €80 a month (about €88.65 with the MEI levy) for the first 12 months. Company directors pay at least about €449.

### What is the regularización of the autónomo quota?

Because Social Security doesn't know your profit in advance, you pay on a bracket you choose. After your Renta, the tax agency sends Social Security your real profit, and it refunds what you overpaid or bills you for what you underpaid. It usually comes about a year after the tax year ends.

### Do I have to file form 130?

Only if less than 70% of your business income had withholding last year (in your first year, this year). Professionals who invoice only Spanish companies usually have 15% withheld and don't file it. Freelancers with private or foreign clients, including most digital nomads, file it every quarter, paying 20% of their cumulative profit minus earlier payments.

### What is the 7% withholding for new autónomos?

Professionals can have 7% withheld from their invoices instead of 15% in the year they start and the next two, as long as they had no professional activity in the year before. You must tell your clients in writing. It only changes how much you prepay; the final tax is the same and is settled in the Renta.

### Can I deduct my home office as an autónomo?

Yes, in proportion to the space you use for work, after declaring it in form 036: part of the rent or mortgage interest, IBI, community fees and insurance. Utilities (water, electricity, gas, internet) count only 30% of that work share, so with a 20% office you deduct 6% of the bills.

### Do I charge IVA to clients outside Spain?

Usually not to businesses. For business clients in another EU country you apply the reverse charge (you need to be in the EU VAT register and file form 349), and services to businesses outside the EU generally carry no Spanish IVA. Selling to private people is different: Spanish IVA usually applies to EU consumers, and above €10,000 a year of digital services you charge their country's VAT through the OSS.

### Do digital nomads on the visa pay autónomo taxes?

Freelancers on the digital nomad visa do: they register as autónomos, pay the Social Security quota (the tarifa plana in the first year), file IRPF and usually form 130 every quarter, since foreign clients don't withhold. Remote employees of a foreign company on the same visa are not autónomos. Some may be able to use the Beckham law special regime; get advice first.

### How can an autónomo legally pay less tax?

Claim every real business expense with a proper invoice, the 5% allowance for hard-to-prove expenses, the 20% start-up reduction in your first two profitable years, the tarifa plana and any regional refund of it, and private health insurance (€500 per person). A pension plan takes up to €5,750 a year off your taxable income, saving about €1,600-2,100 at typical freelance incomes.

### Do I need a gestor as an autónomo?

No, you can file everything yourself online. But the quarterly forms, the Renta and the regularización are easy to get wrong, especially with foreign clients. A gestor for a simple freelance business typically costs €40-80 a month, and the fee is deductible.
